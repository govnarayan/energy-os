# Design method: how to read any solar proposal

Solar companies sell kWh produced. Only kWh used in the house are worth full price. This method replaces annual totals with month-by-month and hour-by-hour math on the buyer's own house.

## Step 1: collect the intake once

What installers ask for:

1. Address, ownership, roof material, age and condition, stories, shade, HOA or permit rules.
2. Main panel rating and age, sub-panels, gas meter location, existing solar or battery.
3. A year of utility data, rate plan, heating, cooling and hot water equipment.

What they do not ask or discuss objectively:

1. The home's load: current and planned HVAC, insulation and ducting, temperature preferences.
2. The utility: electric rate plans (standard, electrification, solar), gas rates, rate inflation or deflation including local minimums and caps.
3. The equipment: inverter technology, brand (installer margin separated from reviews and service), panel reinstall cost at a roof replacement.
4. The real return: breakeven using forecast grid resale and battery capacity, the cost of qualifying for a pitched summer resale credit, annual maintenance.

Load decides the right size, and rates decide what each kWh is worth.

## Step 2: model production against use

1. Use hourly utility data where it exists (Green Button or the utility's equivalent).
2. Estimate production per month from roof area, tilt, direction and shade.
3. Plot both by month. The gap in the worst month sets the size.

## Step 3: size to the worst month

Production peaks in summer and bottoms out in winter. Panels sized to cover the worst month overproduce in summer. Panels sized to a yearly offset percentage overproduce more. Surplus exports earn less than retail, so extra panels add cost, not savings.

## Step 4: ask the nine questions of every proposal

| # | Question | Proposals usually show | Check instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sizing | A yearly offset percentage, sometimes above 100% | Production against use by month, sized to the worst month |
| 2 | Export value | Low resale rates, made up for by a summer utility credit | Nobody pitches exports at the retail rate. Only power used at home earns full price. Test the summer credit: what it pays, on which days, and what it costs to qualify |
| 3 | Battery | Income from selling at the peak | Whether the grid's peak credit beats the retail price you avoid. Size the battery to shift your own evening use |
| 4 | Rate inflation | Savings that grow with yearly utility increases | Past increases do not predict future ones. An electrification rate plan can save more on the bill than solar does, so use it as the ROI baseline |
| 5 | Inverters | Microinverters as always better | They pay off with heavy shade or long, low sun. Otherwise string inverters give the same energy for less |
| 6 | Panels | Watts and brand | Price per usable kWh after losses, plus degradation and warranty terms |
| 7 | EV | "Drive for free" | Spare solar by month. Outside summer afternoons there is little |
| 8 | Financing and incentives | A low monthly payment | Total cost over the term with every lender fee. Which structure: loan, lease or PPA |
| 9 | Future load | Today's bill, plus room for what comes next | Installers do ask about heat pumps and EVs, and utilities allow some oversizing for them. Use the allowance for loads you will add |

## Step 5: price per usable kWh

Divide the system price by the kWh the house will actually use over its life, after losses and degradation. Compare proposals on that number, not on watts or monthly payment.

## Step 6: check the claims

Savings figures often rest on an assumed yearly rise in utility prices, on lender fees folded into the system price, or on incentives that no longer exist. Ask for each assumption in writing. Ask which tax rules apply to the structure you are signing, and check them against the tax authority's own page.

## What this method does not do

It does not give tax or legal advice. It does not replace a site visit for roof condition and shade. It does not know your local tariff until you supply it.
